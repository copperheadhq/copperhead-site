A post on X<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> made the rounds with a tidy picture of a
differential pair going round a U-bend. The inner trace has less distance to cover, so
it arrives about 9.5 ps early. A serpentine of about 1.6 mm on that trace puts it back
in step.

<XPost id="2110776676795953595">
  <p lang="en" dir="ltr">In a differential pair, every bend puts one trace on the inside, which ends up shorter.<br /><br />Two turns at 20 mil pitch ends up being about 63 mil shorter and ~10 ps early. Now the halves don't cancel, and that leaks out as noise and EMI.<br /><br />A small serpentine adds the length back <a href="https://t.co/rLnwI6JNMS">https://t.co/rLnwI6JNMS</a></p>Cornelius Robinson (@CorneliusWrites), <a href="https://x.com/CorneliusWrites/status/2110776676795953595?ref_src=twsrc%5Etfw">October 15, 2026</a>
</XPost>

The geometry is right. The step I wasn't sure about is the one every layout tool takes
for granted: equal length means equal delay. Length tuners that report in picoseconds
still get them from the length. A serpentine adds corners. It pulls one trace away from
its partner and folds back on itself, and none of that shows up in a length report.

So I asked Copperhead to check it with a field solver instead of a ruler.

<YouTube id="FNj2nsk9B3Y" title="Fang: Simulating Serpentine Length Matching | Why Equal Length Is Not Equal Delay" />

## The untuned bend

Two arcs that turn 180 degrees side by side differ in length by π times their spacing,
whatever the radius. At a 20 mil pitch that's 1.596 mm, or 62.8 mil. Multiply by √ε_eff
and divide by the speed of light and you have the skew.

Which ε_eff you use matters. At 3.8 it comes to 10.4 ps. The post's 9.5 ps works out to
ε_eff = 3.18, a perfectly normal number for FR-4 microstrip. Copperhead's stackup is
0.25 mm traces on 0.2 mm of ε_r 4.3, which has a single-line ε_eff of 3.15 by the
Hammerstad-Jensen formula<sup><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup>. So geometry says 9.45 ps.

openEMS said 9.3 ps. For the bend on its own, geometry is within 2 % at a 50 ps edge.

## Test boards

Copperhead built one fixture and changed nothing but the tuning. Every board has the
same stackup, the same launches and the same 12 mm legs.

C, D and F are the usual fix in three styles: two sparse excursions, a dense accordion
of four and one broad trombone. On each, Copperhead solved the amplitude so that P and N
come out exactly the same length, chamfered corners included. C far is C moved next to
the launch. E is a control. It swings both traces out together, which adds delay to the
pair and nothing to the difference between them. KiCad's DRC passed every board before
any of them went into the solver.

## Simulation

openEMS<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup> solves Maxwell's equations around the copper
on a grid of cells. The board sits in a box: the copper on its dielectric, a perfect
conductor underneath as the return plane and absorbing boundaries on the other five
faces so the fields leave instead of reflecting back.

<figure>
  <Image
    src={figModel3d}
    alt="Board B's model in three dimensions, its height drawn four times true scale: the green dielectric slab on the PEC floor, the two gold traces, four red lumped ports from the floor to the copper at the launch, and the dashed edges of the air box whose five open faces are Mur boundaries."
  />
  <figcaption>
    The model of board B. The height is drawn four times its true scale, or the slab would be a line.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Each run sends a pulse into one of the four ports, with the other three sitting there as
50 Ω loads<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup>. Four runs give the full response of the
board, its 4×4 S-matrix. scikit-rf<sup><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup> turns that into
differential and common-mode terms, and from there into what a receiver sees for a 50 ps
edge. All in, Copperhead ran 140 simulations.

## Mesh

The cells are 40 µm across over the copper and grow away from it, 1.24 million of them
for a bent board. The 45-degree corner cuts come out as staircases at that size, which
is the first thing to check.

## Delay efficiency

The obvious suspect is the lengths. Copperhead built a transmission-line model of every
board, calibrated on the solver's own straight pair so its coupled lines are the
solver's coupled lines. It knows every length to the micrometre and predicts 0.2, 0.3
and 0.3 ps for F, C and D. Length isn't what's missing.

What the line model can't see is the corners and the coupling between folds. To pull
those apart Copperhead ran one more pair of boards. Each is a straight pair with 6 mm
added to one trace in three excursions, once with the folds 1.0 mm apart and once at the
0.15 mm clearance rule. Same corners and same added length. They aren't quite the same
board, though. The tight one packs its folds into 2.15 mm against 6.40 mm, so P also
spends less of its run away from N.

Call the delay a tuning actually adds, over what its length says it should add, its
efficiency. The loose serpentine manages 0.84. Packed to the clearance rule it drops to
0.68, and it came out 16 to 24 points lower on every mesh. That fits the fold coupling
Wu and Chao<sup><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup> and Rubin and
Singh<sup><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup> found in single-ended meanders. It doesn't
prove it, because of those section lengths.

The loose serpentine's own shortfall I'd put down to its corners, where the current cuts
across the inside of each turn. That path is shorter than the centre line the length
report measures. No board here changes the corners on their own, so that's my best guess
rather than a result.

## Stripline

Microstrip runs its two modes at different speeds, so some of this could have been a
microstrip quirk. Copperhead put the same copper between two planes and ran it again, on
one mesh only.

It wasn't. Geometry predicts the stripline bend to 0.6 % (10.97 ps against 11.04 ps).
The tunings removed 81, 64 and 42 % of the skew. In microstrip they removed 75, 61 and
44 %.

Tight folds still cost 20 points. Copperhead had written into the first draft of its
report that this penalty should shrink in stripline, because far-end coupling between
parallel lines vanishes there. It doesn't shrink. My best guess, which nothing here
tests, is that neighbouring folds carry current in opposite directions. The coupling
that shortcuts them would then be backward coupling, and stripline has plenty of that.

## Tuning position

C far is the same tuning as C, moved next to the launch. Its skew at the receiver isn't
much worse (4.4 ps against 3.6 ps). Its mode conversion is. Between the tuning and the
bend the pair carries skew the other way, and the two corrections drift out of phase as
the frequency goes up. Read as a skew, C far's grows from 4.1 ps at 5 GHz to 6.5 ps at
25 GHz while C's stays flat. That's one placement against another, not a sweep of the
distance.

## Impedance and return loss

The broad trombone removed the most skew. But its trace leaves its partner for 3 mm and
the differential impedance there jumps to 114 Ω, against 107 Ω for the plain bend. Its
return loss is the worst of the length-matched boards (−16.8 dB against −20.7 dB).

## Eye and common mode

At 16 Gb/s with 35 ps edges, the untuned bend closes the eye from 92.9 % to 91.1 % of
the swing. That's small. The bigger effect is common mode, 13.6 % of the swing peak to
peak at the receiver against 3.3 % for the trombone. A receiver with decent common-mode
rejection won't notice the timing. Whether the common mode becomes an emissions problem
depends on the rest of the board, and none of this models that.

## Sizing from the residual

If each excursion loses a fixed amount, the fix is to put that amount back. Copperhead
lengthened C, D and F by their own leftover skew, converted to length at the bend's
rate. By CAD measure P is now longer than N. The line model says all three overshoot: to
-1.9 ps for F, -3.0 ps for C and -4.5 ps for D.

openEMS put F at 0.15 ps and C at 0.56 ps, so one iteration was enough for those two.
Neither was rerun on other meshes, so the ±0.3 ps from C's mesh check is a loan here,
not a measurement. The dense accordion only came down to 1.9 ps. Each extra millimetre
delivered 62 % of what it should have, against 83 % for C and 92 % for F. My guess is
that stretching its tightly packed verticals tightens the coupling between them too.

## Design guidance

For edge-coupled pairs around this pitch, in microstrip or stripline:

- Trust geometry for the bend and not for the fix. Budget a few picoseconds of residual,
  or size the tuning from a field solve or a coupon.
- Use as few excursions as you can. The loss per excursion shrinks with its size, but
  not as fast as the count grows.
- Keep the folds apart. At the clearance rule they cost 16 to 24 points of efficiency.
- Tune next to the bend, not at the end of the route.
- Keep a broad excursion short or it'll show up on the TDR.
- Swinging the whole pair out does nothing for skew within the pair.

## Why Copperhead runs studies

It's easy to build an AI for PCBs that's judged by the rules it follows. DRC passes, the
length report reads zero, done. On this bend that bar passes a board that still carries
5.2 ps of skew.

Copperhead works the other way round. It treats a rule as a claim to test, runs the
study that tests it and keeps the result as a check with a number on it. This post is
one of those studies from start to finish. Copperhead built the fixtures, ran the 140
solves, caught its own mistakes (below) and wrote the report.

The checks run on real designs through fang<sup><a href="#ref-8">[8]</a></sup>,
Copperhead's hardware kernel. A requirement that needs a simulation gets one. ngspice
takes the circuits and Renode runs the firmware against the board it's written for. A
value that breaks a requirement never reaches the design. One that nothing has measured
yet stays unknown instead of passing.

This study ran on its own scripts, beside fang rather than through it. fang can't ask a
field solver for skew yet, and the residual budget above is the first check I'd want it
to run.

## Errors during the study

The first runs never finished. openEMS stops once the energy in the model has decayed,
and here it stalled well short of the target. Copperhead traced that to the DC content
of the pulse, which leaves a static field behind that never decays. Every run now stops
at a fixed 2 ns instead, after checking that the signals at the ports have fallen at
least 98 dB.

The dense accordion wouldn't converge. On three meshes its skew came out at 4.45, 5.16
and 4.88 ps. The honest bound on that is ±2.1 ps on a 5 ps result. Its 75 µm corner cuts
are only a couple of cells wide. A fourth, finer mesh gave 4.84 ps and settled it at
about 4.8 ps. The coarse mesh had been the outlier.

It also first scored the stripline boards against microstrip's velocity, which made them
look 17 % better than they are. That got caught because the loose stripline serpentine
came out at 0.98, which didn't fit anything else in the study.

## Limitations

All of this is simulation. Nothing was built or measured. It's one trace width and one
pitch in one microstrip and one stripline stackup. The ports are lumped and the return
planes perfect. The copper has no roughness.

Every full-wave number here comes from one solver, openEMS. The line model was
calibrated on its runs, so the two agreeing isn't an independent check. Nor are the
mechanisms pinned down. The fold result is tangled up with section length, and the
corners are a guess by elimination.

The report has a coupon plan for checking it on real boards. The first thing I'd want to
see on a VNA is the dense accordion leaving 5.2 ps where the length report says zero.
