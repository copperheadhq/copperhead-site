The bulb went dim first. A little later it started to flicker. I put in a new one and
was left holding the old one, a Crompton 9 W with a date code of February 2019, not sure
what to do with it. Throwing it out felt like a waste, so I opened it instead.

Part of the fun of taking a product apart is that you might not get it back together.
This one had already been replaced and was headed for the bin, so the worst case was
that it went there in more pieces.

I decided to work from photos alone with no multimeter and without powering it up
again. I pulled it apart and shot both sides of each board with my phone. That came to
54 photos, most of them in ten minutes and the rest a few hours later with a ruler in
the frame.

## Running copperhead backwards

copperhead usually goes from a written brief to a circuit board. Its reverse engineering
framework runs the other way, from photos to the circuit, the parts, the risks and a
record of how sure it is about every claim.

In that record anything read straight off a photo is marked observed. Anything
calculated from those readings is derived, with the working next to it. A judgement call
is inferred and gets a confidence between 0 and 1. When two pieces of evidence disagree
it records both and flags the conflict. It also keeps a list of the 19 things it doesn't
know about this bulb and marks up the photos with the continuity checks that would
answer them.

It went looking online too. As far as it could find, nobody has written up the inside of
a Crompton bulb. The only outside evidence was repair
videos<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup>.
YouTube wouldn't let it download them, so it read what it could from thumbnails, the
first minute of one video and storyboard frames 160 pixels wide.

## Taking it apart

The diffuser dome comes off the housing. Under it is an aluminium cup, and the LED board
sits on that cup on a layer of thermal grease, held by two self-tapping screws. That
board is the part people swap in bulb repair videos, which takes those two screws and
two wires. The driver board hangs on its wires inside the neck with no mounting holes at
all.

The plastic rim cracked while I was prying it open and the big inductor came off the
driver board on the way out. Both would have mattered more if I were putting it back
together.

## A driver with no big capacitor on the mains side

The driver is about 48 by 24 mm with roughly 20 parts on one copper layer. The bigger
through-hole parts sit on one face and the small surface-mount parts are glued to the
copper face, so the board gets soldered in a single pass over a solder wave. Red glue
dots also mark a few empty footprints: spare positions for alternative parts and one for
trimming the LED current.

Mains comes in through a 68 Ω fusible resistor, which does the job of both a fuse and an
inrush limiter. Next is a 510 V varistor across the line to catch spikes, an MB10F
bridge rectifier<sup><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup> and a small π filter made of two 0.1
µF film capacitors around a drum inductor. There is no big electrolytic capacitor on the
mains side and no X or Y capacitors.

Without one, the controller chip runs straight off the rectified mains and shapes the
current it draws to follow the sine wave, which is how the bulb gets the power factor
above 0.9 printed on its housing. It works because the LED string is low voltage. The
chip is a buck converter, so it can draw power only while the mains is above the LED
voltage, about 81 V here against a 325 V peak. It goes quiet for about 14 degrees
either side of each zero crossing. The lamp's big capacitor, 68 µF at 100 V, sits across
the LEDs instead and smooths what reaches them.

On this side the chip's supply and loop capacitors are placed right at its pins, and its
drain pins sit on a big copper pour that spreads the heat. The top half of the feedback
divider is two 120 kΩ resistors in series where you'd expect one 240 kΩ. That looks odd
until you work out that it sees about 244 V on every switching cycle, more than the 200
V a single resistor of that size is rated for.

## Part markings and date codes

Resistors and capacitors carry short codes, and once the photo is sharp enough the
decoding is mechanical. `2R0` is 2.0 Ω. `124` is 12 followed by four zeros, so 120 kΩ.
`07D511K` is a 7 mm disc rated 510 V<sup><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup>. The fusible
resistor uses colour bands instead, and blue, grey, black and gold read as 68 Ω at 5%.

The codes also date the bulb, if I'm reading them right. The driver's board name ends in
180425, which looks like 25 April 2018. The last line on the chip reads week 46, which
fits November 2018. The housing says February 2019. So this board revision is dated
about ten months before the bulb was built.

## The LEDs run hot

The LED board's silkscreen says `NBL7W9S1P2835V1.0`, which reads as a 7 W board with
nine 2835 LEDs in series. Working back from the driver, it's being fed about 8.1 W.
That's around 16% over the board's own label, close to 0.9 W per LED.

It's a lot of heat for a small square of board. About 5.7 W of it has to leave through
a smear of thermal grease, into the aluminium cup and out through a plastic housing.
Nothing in the circuit watches the LED temperature. copperhead also flags the output
capacitor, a 105 °C part sealed in the neck, as the part most likely to limit how long
the bulb lasts.

The middle LED has a small black burn mark over one of its dies. I can't prove that's
what made my bulb dim and flicker because I never powered it again to check. But I
found a repair video of another flickering Crompton
bulb<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> that shows the same board with the same kind of
burn on a different LED.

## Pinning down the chip

Working from the circuit around it, the first pass said it was a Bright Power
Semiconductor part from the BP2335 family<sup><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup>. Then I
read the marking by eye. I read it several times and got a different answer almost every
time: BP2836, then BP2336 or BP2338, then BP2636. The photos weren't any better and read
BP2835, BP2865 or BP2885 depending on the light.

copperhead took my first read and rebuilt everything around BP2836 until it noticed
BP2836 only comes in a through-hole package<sup><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup> and this
chip is surface mount. Next it settled on BP2336J and marked it verified because the
second and third lines of the marking were in the right format.

That was too strong and copperhead pulled it back. The same maker prints that format on
several different chips<sup><a href="#ref-8">[8]</a><a href="#ref-9">[9]</a></sup>, so a
matching format only identifies the maker.

The record now calls it BP2336J and the circuit around it backs that up. The evidence
alone puts it at 0.55, and I had it commit at 0.75 so the work could keep moving. A
couple of beeps from a multimeter on continuity will settle it.

## Numbers I don't like

If an LED fails open, the chip is meant to shut down when the output reaches about 108
V<sup><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup>. The capacitor holding that output is rated 100 V.
That depends on my reading of three feedback resistors that nobody has traced yet, but
if it's right, a dead LED would push that capacitor 8% past its rating before the chip
steps in.

Surge is the other gap. The varistor is rated 510 V, the switch inside the chip is rated
500 V and there's only 0.2 µF on the rectified mains between them. Photos cannot show
whether the varistor catches a spike before the chip does. Crompton's current 9 W bulbs
advertise 4 kV surge protection<sup><a href="#ref-10">[10]</a></sup>, and this 2019 one
prints no surge rating at all.

With no big capacitor on the mains side, copperhead also expects 17 to 25% flicker at
100 Hz, which is too fast to see directly. It rates that figure as a weak estimate.

## What copperhead would change

copperhead's verdict is that the design was built to meet the standards as cheaply as
possible, with reliability second. It meets the power factor and efficiency it has
to<sup><a href="#ref-11">[11]</a><a href="#ref-12">[12]</a></sup> on very few parts, and
the LEDs run hot against the board's label. The changes it suggests are small. Use
a 160 V output capacitor. Either add a few LEDs or swap the sense resistor for 2.4 Ω,
which brings the current down to about 83 mA for roughly 15% less light. Run a proper
surge test at 1 and 2.5 kV before touching the varistor.

Each of these findings also becomes a rule copperhead can check on a new design before
anyone builds it:

- the output capacitor is rated at least 1.1 times the overvoltage trip
- LED power is checked against the LED's own rating, never the board's label
- a driver with no bulk capacitance on the mains side gets a flicker estimate
- every resistor in a high-voltage divider is checked against its working voltage
- the varistor's clamp voltage is checked against the switch's breakdown voltage

## What I still don't know

Everything here came from photos, so none of it has been measured yet. The copper is
hidden under solid green solder mask, so the 16 connections in the circuit come from the
chip's reference design. Only three of them can be seen in a photo. The LED
current depends on the chip identification, and the 8.1 W assumes the driver is 90%
efficient. It's also one bulb from 2019, and Crompton's newer ones may be built
differently. One of the repair videos already shows a Crompton 9 W driver of a different
design<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup>.

The next step is a multimeter. copperhead has already marked about 40 continuity checks
on the photos, to be done with the bulb unplugged. It also wants a sharper photo of the
chip. Measuring it with power on is a job for someone with an isolation transformer.
Every part of this lamp, the LED board included, sits at mains voltage when it's
switched on.
